This is a strong pick. I would make it **one clean puzzle** rather than a broad “traffic and society” video.

The best title is:

# Why Adding Roads Can Make Traffic Worse

The shape of the video:

**A city tries to fix traffic.**
It adds a shortcut.
Everyone behaves rationally.
The result gets worse for everyone.

That gives you a very clean story arc.

---

# Full 4–5 minute script

## Scene 1 — The obvious solution

**Visual**

Black background or simple map-style grid.

Two points appear:

**HOME** on the left.
**DOWNTOWN** on the right.

A crowd of small car dots waits at Home.

Two routes appear: an upper route and a lower route.

**Voiceover**

Every morning, thousands of people drive from the suburbs into the city.

There are two main routes.

The top road.

And the bottom road.

Neither one is perfect. Both get crowded. Both are annoying.

So the city looks at the map and thinks: this is obvious.

Add one new road.

A shortcut.

More roads should mean less traffic.

Right?

**Visual**

A glowing dotted shortcut appears between the middle of the top route and the middle of the bottom route.

Pause.

Text on screen:

**More roads = less traffic?**

---

## Scene 2 — The road network

**Visual**

Simplify the map into a clean diagram.

Four nodes:

Left: **Start**
Right: **End**
Top middle: **A**
Bottom middle: **B**

Roads:

Start → A
A → End
Start → B
B → End

Show 4,000 tiny cars at Start.

Now label the roads.

Top-left road:

**Time = cars / 100**

Top-right road:

**45 min**

Bottom-left road:

**45 min**

Bottom-right road:

**Time = cars / 100**

**Voiceover**

Here is the whole city, stripped down to its math.

There are 4,000 drivers.

Some roads have a fixed travel time. No matter how many people take them, they take 45 minutes.

Other roads depend on traffic. If 1,000 people take the road, it takes 10 minutes. If 2,000 people take it, it takes 20 minutes. If 4,000 people take it, it takes 40 minutes.

So the travel time is just:

cars divided by 100.

Simple.

Now the drivers choose their route.

Nobody is trying to ruin traffic. Nobody is making a mistake.

Each person just wants to get to the city as fast as possible.

---

## Scene 3 — Before the shortcut

**Visual**

Show half the cars going top route, half going bottom route.

Top route:

Start → A: 2,000 cars → **20 min**
A → End: **45 min**

Total: **65 min**

Bottom route:

Start → B: **45 min**
B → End: 2,000 cars → **20 min**

Total: **65 min**

Put both totals beside the routes.

**Top route: 65 min**
**Bottom route: 65 min**

**Voiceover**

Before the shortcut exists, the traffic naturally splits.

About half the drivers take the top route.

The other half take the bottom route.

On the top route, the first road has 2,000 cars, so it takes 20 minutes. Then the fixed road takes 45 minutes.

Total: 65 minutes.

On the bottom route, the fixed road comes first: 45 minutes. Then the crowded road takes 20 minutes.

Again: 65 minutes.

So the system balances.

If you switch routes, you do not really gain anything.

Everyone is stuck with a 65 minute commute.

Not great.

But stable.

**Visual**

Text appears:

**Before shortcut: everyone takes 65 minutes**

---

## Scene 4 — The city adds a shortcut

**Visual**

The new road appears between A and B.

Label it:

**0 min shortcut**

Make it visually satisfying: a glowing bridge, tunnel, or diagonal connector.

**Voiceover**

Now the city builds a new road.

A beautiful shortcut from A to B.

And to make the effect as dramatic as possible, let’s say this shortcut is basically instant.

Zero minutes.

Surely this can only help.

After all, nobody is being forced to use it.

It just gives drivers one more option.

More options should make things better.

**Visual**

Text:

**A new option appears.**

Then pause.

Text changes:

**So what do drivers do?**

---

## Scene 5 — The selfish logic

**Visual**

Focus on one driver at Start.

Highlight their options.

From Start, compare:

Start → A has traffic-dependent time.
Start → B is fixed 45 minutes.

At the start of the day, the driver thinks:

Start → A is probably faster than 45.

So they choose Start → A.

Then from A, compare:

A → End is fixed 45 minutes.
A → B → End uses the free shortcut, then the traffic-dependent road.

If the last road is less than 45 minutes, the shortcut path looks better.

Show the driver taking:

Start → A → B → End

**Voiceover**

Now look at the decision from one driver’s point of view.

At the start, they can go to A or B.

The road to A depends on traffic, but it is usually better than the fixed 45 minute road to B.

So they choose A.

Then, once they reach A, they face another choice.

They could go straight to the end in 45 minutes.

Or they could take the free shortcut down to B, and then use the final traffic-dependent road.

That looks better too.

So the driver takes the shortcut.

And here is the trap.

Every other driver thinks the same way.

**Visual**

One car becomes many.

All 4,000 cars flow through:

Start → A → B → End

The shortcut glows. Then the two traffic-dependent roads become red.

---

## Scene 6 — The paradox

**Visual**

Calculate the new route.

Start → A: 4,000 cars → **40 min**

A → B: **0 min**

B → End: 4,000 cars → **40 min**

Total:

**80 min**

Big text:

**New commute: 80 minutes**

Then compare:

Before: **65 min**
After: **80 min**

**Voiceover**

Now all 4,000 drivers are using the same path.

The first traffic-dependent road has everyone on it, so it takes 40 minutes.

The shortcut takes zero minutes.

The final traffic-dependent road also has everyone on it, so it takes 40 minutes.

Total commute:

80 minutes.

The city added a road.

Nobody made a wrong turn.

Everyone chose the route that looked best for them.

And somehow, everyone is now 15 minutes slower than before.

This is Braess’s paradox.

Adding capacity to a network can make the whole network worse when people choose routes selfishly.

**Visual**

Text:

**Before: 65 min**
**After adding shortcut: 80 min**

Then:

**The shortcut made the system worse.**

---

## Scene 7 — Why nobody switches

**Visual**

Show one annoyed driver on the 80 minute shortcut route.

They consider switching back to the old top route.

Top route now would be:

Start → A: still 40 min
A → End: 45 min
Total: 85 min

Then lower route:

Start → B: 45 min
B → End: still 40 min
Total: 85 min

Show both as worse than 80.

**Voiceover**

The strangest part is that once everyone is stuck in the bad pattern, no single driver can fix it.

Suppose one person tries to avoid the shortcut and take the old top route.

They still have to use the crowded first road, which now takes 40 minutes.

Then they take the fixed 45 minute road.

That is 85 minutes.

Even worse.

The old bottom route is also 85 minutes.

So each individual driver looks at the situation and thinks:

The shortcut is still my best option.

Individually, that is true.

Collectively, it is terrible.

**Visual**

Text:

**Best for me**
appears over one car.

Then zoom out to all cars:

**Worse for everyone**

---

## Scene 8 — The bigger idea

**Visual**

The road diagram transforms into other networks:

Traffic map.
Internet routing.
People choosing checkout lines.
Data packets moving through servers.
Crowds choosing exits.
Maybe even “players” in a game.

**Voiceover**

This is why the paradox matters.

It is not really about one weird road.

It is about networks where everyone chooses what seems best from their own point of view.

Drivers choose the fastest route.

Data packets move through the internet.

People choose lines, platforms, shortcuts, strategies.

And sometimes, when every individual optimizes for themselves, the system does not optimize for everyone.

The invisible hand gets stuck in traffic.

**Visual**

Return to the original road diagram.

Remove the shortcut.

The flow splits again.

65 minutes returns.

**Voiceover**

The solution can even feel absurd.

Sometimes, removing a road can improve traffic.

Not because less infrastructure is magically better.

Because the structure of the network changes the choices people make.

A road is not just extra space.

It is an incentive.

And incentives can redirect the whole system.

---

## Scene 9 — Ending

**Visual**

Clean final image:

A city map from above.

A bright new shortcut appears.

Cars rush toward it.

The whole map turns red.

Then the title appears:

**Why Adding Roads Can Make Traffic Worse**

**Voiceover**

So the next time a city says it will fix traffic by adding one more road, one more lane, one more shortcut…

The question is not only:

Does this create more space?

The deeper question is:

What will everyone do once that space exists?

Because in a network, a new path does not just change the map.

It changes the game.

# Strong visual style

Use a **minimal map + glowing flow** style.

The whole video can be built from one diagram:

```text
        A
      /   \
Start     End
      \   /
        B
```

Then add the shortcut:

```text
        A
      / | \
Start  |  End
      \ | /
        B
```

Make the cars tiny dots flowing along edges. The important thing is not realism. It is watching the system “snap” into a worse equilibrium.
